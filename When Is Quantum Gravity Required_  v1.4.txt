When Is Quantum Gravity Required? 
A Finite Experimental Adjudication Framework 
1. Introduction
Quantum gravity is usually approached as a problem of theoretical construction. General Relativity and quantum theory are both extraordinarily successful within their established domains, yet their coexistence leaves open a basic empirical question: under what conditions would gravity itself require a quantum description?
Laboratory proposals have made that question experimentally concrete. Gravitationally induced entanglement, measurement backaction, process nonclassicality, semiclassical nonlinearities, stochastic diffusion, and related effects all offer ways to probe gravitational interactions involving controlled quantum systems. But those signatures do not all establish the same thing. An observed effect may exclude one classical model while leaving another intact. Entanglement may rule out a restricted local mediator class while a broader hybrid theory survives. A nonlinear or measurement-conditioned model may not satisfy the same channel assumptions used by a process witness. An apparently gravitational nonclassical signal may instead be carried by another quantum interaction. And even a decisive failure of classical gravity would not identify the microscopic theory that replaces it.
The empirical problem is therefore narrower than “detect quantum gravity” and stronger than “observe an effect consistent with quantum gravity.” It is to determine what evidence would show that no physically serious classical gravitational description remains adequate for the same observed effect and associated data.
This paper develops a finite adjudication framework for that question. The central criterion separates four requirements that are often compressed into one: the source and probe must independently support the quantum description used in the inference; the relevant quantum structure must be shown to matter to the measured effect; the effect must be attributable to gravity; and the predeclared classical comparison class must fail to reproduce the complete registered data. Only their conjunction supports the conclusion that a quantum-gravity resolution is required at the tested scope.
The hardest part is the final requirement. “Classical gravity” cannot be reduced to one semiclassical mean-field model, one metric formulation, or one local-mediator assumption. Current classical and hybrid proposals already relax different parts of that picture through stochastic dynamics, nonlocal or correlated structure, modified quantum interfaces, measurement-conditioned gravity, or alternate quantum-information paths through matter. A useful experiment must therefore test not only a preferred classical model but also the physical changes by which serious alternatives evade it.
The framework developed here organizes those alternatives by experimentally distinguishable structure rather than by theory name. A model that leaves the standard local classical-process class must expose what changed—an alternate quantum carrier, nonlocal or correlated mediation, a modified quantum interface, or an irreversible or stochastic classical-quantum dynamics—and the experiment must include an independent discriminator for that change. The comparison is made against one predeclared data package and one common treatment of uncertainty.
The proposed realization uses two massive path degrees of freedom and a finite preparation, readout, control, and geometry-time architecture. It tests preparation equivalence before using a linear process description, compares coherent and deliberately dephased inputs, pairs target and gravity-suppressed process data, bounds alternate quantum channels, and measures the accompanying coherence and noise trajectory. Candidate classical descriptions are then required to fit the complete registered data with one physically consistent parameterization.
The resulting criterion is scoped rather than ontological:
* independently established quantum structure
* ∧ quantum structure necessary for the tested effect
* ∧ independent gravity attribution
* ∧ failure of the predeclared classical comparison class
* ⇒ quantum-gravity resolution required at the declared scope.
The conclusion does not select gravitons, quantum geometry, discreteness, strings, loops, a persistent metric, or any other microscopic realization. It establishes only that the tested observations can no longer be reproduced by the classical gravitational descriptions included under the declared comparison rule. A later classical representation that faithfully reproduces the same complete data would reopen that conclusion.
To make the framework physically concrete, the paper also examines a screened, trapped mesoscopic-interferometer realization. A representative point near 10⁻¹⁴ kg masses, tens-of-micrometres interaction separation, several-micrometre path splitting, and approximately second-scale interaction times yields a gravitational phase large enough to make the comparison experimentally meaningful. The dominant open requirements are instead mesoscopic coherence, residual-gas decoherence, spin and motional coherence in the target object, magnetic and surface backgrounds, alternate-channel bounds, and final process-level statistical power.
The aim is therefore not to specify what quantum gravity is. It is to define a finite empirical route for determining when an adequate classical gravitational description is no longer available and a quantum-gravity resolution has become necessary.
2. The Empirical Requirement for Quantum Gravity
A laboratory test of quantum gravity has to answer a more demanding question than whether quantum matter can participate in a gravitational experiment. The quantum character of matter is already established. Nor is it enough, by itself, to observe a correlation or interference effect that is difficult to reproduce with one familiar semiclassical model. The relevant question is narrower and stronger: under what empirical conditions would the same observed gravitational effect and associated data no longer admit any adequate classical gravitational description?
This distinction matters because several experimentally interesting statements that are often grouped together are not equivalent. A source may be prepared in a quantum state without its quantum coherence playing any load-bearing role in the gravitational effect under study. A measured nonclassical correlation may be genuine while the relevant interaction has not yet been isolated from other quantum-information channels. A particular classical model may fail while another classical representation survives. Conversely, the failure of a classical gravitational description does not determine what microscopic theory must replace it. A useful experimental criterion for quantum gravity therefore has to separate these questions rather than allowing one of them to stand in for the others.
Gravity-mediated entanglement proposals made this issue experimentally concrete. Bose et al. and Marletto and Vedral showed how two quantum masses interacting gravitationally could, under appropriate assumptions, generate entanglement whose observation would place strong pressure on descriptions in which the mediating gravitational degree of freedom carries only classical information.[1] That insight remains central to laboratory quantum-gravity research. It establishes that the quantum information carried through a gravitational interaction can itself become an observable target.
The stronger inference, however, depends on the comparison class. Subsequent work has shown why the qualification matters. The Diósi-Penrose model can generate entanglement in part of its parameter space, and broader Markovian hybrid classical-quantum models can do so when additional nonlocal or correlated structure is present.[2] A different route appears when matter is described as a quantum field: Aziz and Howl have shown, within their framework, that quantum information can be transmitted through matter-sector processes even while gravity remains classical.[3] These results do not make gravitationally induced entanglement uninformative. They show instead that entanglement is a discriminator only relative to the physical assumptions defining the classical alternative being tested.
The same lesson appears from the opposite direction. Lami, Pedernales, and Plenio derive tests of gravitational process nonclassicality that do not require the interaction to generate entanglement at all.[4] Hanif et al. formulate a measurement-disturbance route, while Chen and Giacomini identify gravity-sensitive quantum effects extending beyond the simplest Newton-potential phase picture.[5] Taken together, these lines of work suggest that the underlying empirical object is not one privileged signature. It is the class of physical descriptions capable of reproducing the complete set of observations produced by the experiment.
The analysis therefore separates four empirical questions.
First, the degree of freedom entering the experiment must independently support a quantum description at the scale and accuracy relevant to the test. This is a domain-entry requirement, not a conclusion drawn from the gravitational result itself. If the source or probe has not been shown to support the quantum states, coherence, and operations used in the inference, a failure of a classical gravitational model cannot by itself supply that missing evidence.
Second, the quantum structure must matter to the particular effect being tested. The mere presence of a quantum system is insufficient. If deliberate removal of the relevant coherence leaves the claimed gravitational signature unchanged within the experimental resolution, then that signature has not established that quantum structure is necessary for the effect. A useful experiment must therefore distinguish “quantum system present” from “quantum structure physically required for this effect.”
Third, the effect must be attributable to gravity rather than to an unaccounted quantum or classical interaction. This requires more than observing the expected scale of a signal. Electromagnetic coupling, Casimir or patch-potential forces, mechanical and magnetic channels, matter-field processes, control leakage, and other apparatus-specific interactions must either be suppressed, independently calibrated, or carried explicitly as competing explanations. A nonclassical source-probe process does not become evidence about gravity until the gravity attribution itself survives those controls.
Only then does the hardest question arise: can any admitted classical gravitational representation still reproduce the same observations and relations?
Here “classical gravitational representation” is deliberately broader than General Relativity in its standard metric form. A meaningful exclusion claim cannot be defined so narrowly that a reformulation, stochastic extension, measurement-feedback model, nonlinear semiclassical theory, nonlocal hybrid, or other physically serious classical construction escapes merely because it was not included in the initial comparison. The comparison class must be fixed by the physical role being tested, not by allegiance to one representation. General Relativity remains the established classical theory of gravity in its domain of validity, but failure of one GR-based approximation is not equivalent to failure of classical gravity as a class.
This last requirement changes the logical shape of the experiment. The target is not simply a prediction S for which one quantum model gives S and one classical model does not. The target is a confidence-valid experimental region whose relevant data are incompatible with the full admitted classical comparison class. Schematically, if ℛα denotes the confidence-valid region allowed by the data and 𝒦C the predeclared feasible region of the admitted classical gravitational descriptions, the decisive exclusion has the form
ℛα ∩ 𝒦C = ∅.
The difficulty is therefore transferred to the construction of 𝒦C. It must be broad enough that its exclusion means what the paper says it means, while remaining physically defined enough to be testable. A comparison class made arbitrarily broad after the experiment could never be excluded; a comparison class chosen too narrowly before the experiment would yield a conclusion stronger than the evidence. The coverage of the classical class is consequently part of the empirical requirement rather than an invisible assumption.
The resulting quantum-gravity criterion can be written compactly as
* independently established quantum structure
* ∧ quantum structure load-bearing for the tested effect
* ∧ independent gravity attribution
* ∧ failure of every admitted classical gravitational representation
* ⇒ quantum-gravity resolution required at the declared scope.
The scope clause is essential. The conclusion does not state that every gravitational degree of freedom is quantum in every regime. It states that, for the observations and relations actually tested, the admitted classical descriptions have ceased to be adequate. Physics must then supply a resolution containing whatever quantum gravitational structure is required to account for those observations.
Nothing further follows automatically. In particular, the criterion does not select gravitons, quantum geometry, discrete spacetime, strings, loops, a persistent metric, or any other proposed microscopic ontology. Those are questions for subsequent theory and experiment. The result sought here is prior to that competition: establishing whether the classical side of the comparison has been exhausted strongly enough that a quantum-gravity answer is no longer optional.
This distinction also gives the criterion a straightforward response to future scientific change. Suppose a laboratory result excludes every classical representation admitted at the time, but a later theory identifies a new classical construction that faithfully reproduces the same data and satisfies the same experimental constraints. The classical-representability question reopens. If improved controls later show that an apparent signal came from a nongravitational quantum channel, the gravity attribution reopens. Conversely, if a previously viable classical alternative acquires a measurable consequence and that consequence is excluded, the comparison class narrows. The conclusion follows the evidence supporting its premises rather than becoming insulated from later evidence.
The remainder of this paper develops the experimental consequences of this criterion. The central difficulty is no longer to identify one dramatic signature that can be called “quantum gravity.” It is to construct a finite experiment in which the major physically serious classical alternatives are tested within one declared physical system, one precommitted data architecture, and one common evidential standard.
3. The Classical Comparison Class
The criterion developed above places most of the experimental difficulty on the classical side of the comparison. It is relatively straightforward to specify a particular semiclassical model and ask whether a measurement disagrees with it. It is much harder to justify the stronger statement that no admissible classical gravitational description remains. That statement requires a comparison class broad enough to survive changes of representation while still being constrained enough to make definite experimental predictions.
A useful starting point is the familiar local classical-process picture. The two quantum systems may retain arbitrary local quantum structure, including coherence, local memory, and quantum measurement. The gravitational interaction between them, however, carries only classical information. The most general process of this kind may contain stochasticity, feedback, adaptive control, classical memory, and multiple rounds of communication. What it may not contain is an undeclared quantum system carrying information between the two quantum endpoints. For a bounded interaction window, such processes lie within the class generated by local quantum operations and classical communication and therefore within the larger class of separable quantum channels:
𝒦CM ⊆ 𝒦LOCC ⊆ 𝒦SEP.
This containment gives an experimentally useful outer boundary. Data incompatible with a conservative separable-channel region are necessarily incompatible with the narrower local classical-process hypothesis. The converse does not hold: compatibility with the outer region does not establish that gravity is classical. The outer region is used only because its exclusion is sufficient.
This standard class is not broad enough to settle the full problem. Current classical and hybrid gravitational theories show several distinct ways in which its assumptions can be relaxed. The important point for experiment design is that those relaxations are not cost-free. Each changes some other observable feature of the physical description. The task is therefore not to anticipate every conceivable future theory by name, but to identify what a theory has to change in order to escape the standard process class and then attach an independent experimental discriminator to that change.
One route is to leave the gravitational sector classical while allowing another quantum degree of freedom to transport the relevant information. This possibility becomes particularly clear when matter is treated in quantum field theory. Aziz and Howl have shown within their framework that local theories with classical gravity can nevertheless generate quantum communication and entanglement through matter-sector processes.[6] The resulting entanglement is then not evidence that the gravitational field itself carried quantum information. The experiment has instead revealed that its source–probe partition contained another quantum route.
This does not make the alternative experimentally unconstrained. The additional matter-sector contribution has its own dependence on mass, separation, interaction time, and other parameters. In the framework studied by Aziz and Howl, that dependence differs from the leading phase expected from the ordinary quantum-gravity process considered in the same geometry.[7] A robust experiment can therefore calculate, suppress, or independently bound the alternate quantum channel. The appropriate conclusion is not that such bypasses make quantum gravity untestable, but that gravity attribution has to include them.
A second route is to abandon local factorization. A nominally classical model may contain pre-correlated measurement records, nonlocal interactions, globally correlated noise, or another structure that does not decompose into local endpoint–gravity interactions linked only by ordinary classical communication. Such theories can evade the simplest mediator argument because they give up part of the physical architecture on which that argument rests.
The Diósi-Penrose model provides a concrete example. Trillo and Navascués showed that its dynamics can generate entanglement between two particles, but only in a restricted part of parameter space. In the geometry they study, entanglement appears below a critical separation determined by the model parameter, while the long-time dynamics drives the system toward separability.[8] Angeli and Carlesso reach a related conclusion for broader Markovian hybrid classical-quantum models, identifying nonlocal structure as central to the entangling cases they analyze.[9] These results are important precisely because they do not leave the experiment with an unrestricted escape clause. A nonlocal or correlated classical model predicts a particular dependence on separation, time, decoherence, and its own model parameters. Measuring that trajectory is stronger than asking whether entanglement was present at one operating point.
A third route changes the quantum interface itself. Standard process tomography assumes that the experimentally relevant preparation mixtures and their subsequent evolution are described by an ordinary linear quantum map over the tested domain. State-dependent semiclassical theories need not respect that assumption. The Schrödinger-Newton equation, for example, is nonlinear in the quantum state, and current experimental programs seek its predicted state-dependent mechanical and interferometric effects directly.[10] A recent torsion-balance experiment reported no evidence for the targeted semiclassical signature at its achieved sensitivity, illustrating that these theories can be confronted through their own dynamical consequences rather than through a generic entanglement test.[11]
This distinction matters methodologically. Suppose two physically different randomization procedures prepare the same density operator according to ordinary quantum mechanics. If the subsequent gravitational evolution depends on which decomposition was used, the experiment has found a failure of the standard mixture relation over the tested domain. In that situation it would be incorrect to continue applying a linear-channel exclusion theorem and then interpret its failure as evidence against classical gravity. The channel description itself has become part of what is being tested. A sound adjudication therefore checks the relevant preparation equivalences before using process-classicality results downstream.
Measurement-conditioned semiclassical models sharpen the same lesson from another direction. Miki et al. construct a framework in which the classical gravitational field may be sourced by information obtained through actual measurements or environmental records rather than solely through a quantum expectation value. Their causal conditional Schrödinger-Newton model can reproduce correlations that imitate some steady-state signatures normally associated with quantum mediation.[12] The model is not an arbitrary loophole: its dependence on the available classical measurement record creates additional intervention and time-dependence tests. Delaying, suppressing, or altering the information supplied to the classical gravitational channel changes the predicted behavior. The escape from the simplest classical-mediator picture therefore exposes another experimentally controllable variable.
A fourth issue concerns what is meant by calling the gravity-side process “classical.” The label cannot by itself decide the comparison. A mathematical formulation may use Hilbert-space methods, auxiliary phases, noncommuting operators, hidden variables, or other structures while its authors still identify the relevant physical sector as classical. Conversely, unfamiliar mathematical machinery does not by itself make a theory quantum. For the present purpose the relevant question is operational: can the complete load-bearing interaction be represented by positive classical states and classical information while preserving the experimentally relevant relations?
This is where the comparison class must be disciplined but not artificially narrow. A candidate theory does not remain a classical counterexample merely because its gravitational sector is called classical. The part of the theory that actually carries the source–probe interaction has to admit the classical representation being claimed. If it does, then the experiment must test the additional structure by which it escaped the standard local class—for example, nonlocality or a modified quantum interface. If it does not, then it is not a classical realization of the observed effect in the sense being tested here. This avoids settling a physical question by terminology.
Irreversibility provides a further constraint that cuts across these alternatives. Galley, Giacomini, and Selby proved within a generalized-probabilistic framework that four properties cannot coexist: fully nonclassical matter, backreaction from matter into a classical gravitational system, reversible interaction, and classicality of the gravitational system.[13] If quantum matter remains genuinely nonclassical and gravity receives information from it, a classical gravitational description must therefore give up reversibility or identify which premise of the theorem does not apply.
Several consistent classical-quantum gravity programs accept this price explicitly. Oppenheim’s postquantum classical-gravity framework is stochastic, and the coupling between quantum matter and the classical degrees of freedom produces decoherence together with diffusion in the classical sector.[14] Oppenheim, Sparaciari, Šoda, and Weller-Davies derive a quantitative decoherence–diffusion tradeoff for broad consistent classical-quantum dynamics: suppressing one side of the tradeoff constrains the other.[15] More recent work has developed experimentally accessible consequences of this structure, including correlations in quantum matter and strain spectra associated with fluctuations in geodesic deviation.[16] These are not generic predictions of every possible classical theory of gravity. They demonstrate instead that important consistent classical alternatives expose measurable costs that can be carried into an experimental comparison.
The same care is required when the classical theory is reformulated more broadly. Oppenheim and Weller-Davies have constructed a covariant path-integral framework in which quantum fields backreact on classical fields, including a classical spacetime metric, while the overall evolution remains completely positive. In that framework the classical field itself cannot generate entanglement.[17] Such a theory should not be identified automatically with one earlier master-equation model, nor should its classicality be dismissed because its formulation differs from familiar semiclassical equations. It has to be tested against the same experimental data and on the observables that its structure actually constrains.
The classical comparison problem therefore becomes finite in a useful sense without pretending that the space of future theories is finite. A theory that escapes the standard local classical-process class has to reveal what changed: another quantum carrier has entered the source–probe path; local factorization has been abandoned; the ordinary quantum interface has been modified; or the allegedly classical interaction no longer admits the operational classical representation being claimed. A genuinely classical theory that also receives information from nonclassical matter must additionally account for the corresponding irreversibility or identify why the relevant no-go assumptions fail.
This organization determines the experimental strategy developed in the next section. The experiment need not assign one preferred statistic to every named theory. It needs a common data package rich enough that each physically admitted alternative is forced to answer on the feature by which it survives. A local classical process must survive the process-classicality test. An alternate quantum channel must survive independent bypass bounds. A nonlocal or correlated model must survive the measured geometry and time dependence. A nonlinear theory must survive preparation-consistency tests and its own state-dependent predictions. A stochastic classical theory must survive the measured decoherence, diffusion, and correlation budget.
The comparison is exhausted only when no admitted classical representation can fit the complete relevant data under one physically allowed parameterization. Different portions of the data cannot be assigned to mutually incompatible parameter choices simply to keep the theory alive. The experiment therefore asks a more stringent question than whether each observable can be explained separately: can one classical gravitational description reproduce the complete registered data from the same experiment?
That requirement is what turns the classical comparison from an indefinitely expandable catalogue into an empirical problem.
4. An Empirical Adjudication Protocol
The comparison developed above changes what the experiment has to do. It is not enough to optimize one witness against one classical model. The experiment must produce a common body of data in which the assumptions supporting the primary inference are themselves tested and in which the major surviving classical alternatives remain exposed to independent discrimination. The appropriate object is therefore not one measurement setting but one bounded, predeclared family of interventions performed on the same physical source–probe system at the same declared experimental scope.
The interventions may vary preparation, interaction time, branch geometry, readout basis, or deliberately applied controls. What may not vary after the outcome is known is the physical question being asked, the admissible model families, the statistical rule by which they are judged, or the parameter ranges each model is allowed to use. A candidate explanation that survives one portion of the data only by adopting parameter values incompatible with another portion has not explained the experiment. The relevant comparison is joint.
This requirement is particularly important because the strongest process-level inference rests on assumptions that are themselves physical. A local classical-process model can be represented as local quantum operations at the two endpoints connected only by classical information. Over a bounded interaction window, this places the resulting endpoint process within the LOCC class and therefore within the broader set of separable channels.[18] Excluding a conservative separable-channel region can consequently exclude the narrower local classical-process hypothesis. But that inference is legitimate only if the experimentally realized source and probe actually admit the finite quantum-channel description on which the comparison is built.
The protocol therefore begins by testing the interface before using it.
Consider two different laboratory randomization procedures that, under ordinary quantum mechanics, prepare the same density operator. A simple example is an equal hidden mixture of the two path states and an equal hidden mixture of two orthogonal coherent superpositions. If the randomization record is unavailable to the subsequent interaction, both procedures represent the same maximally mixed path state. Their later statistics should therefore be identical under any ordinary linear quantum process, apart from experimental uncertainty.
Schematically,
½(|L⟩⟨L| + |R⟩⟨R|)
= ½(|+⟩⟨+| + |−⟩⟨−|).
The equality is useful because the two sides are not merely two mathematical decompositions written after the fact. They can be implemented as genuinely different hidden laboratory procedures. If those procedures produce distinguishable output distributions after the same interaction window, then the standard mixture relation has failed over the tested domain. A linear-channel theorem should not then be used to infer the nature of gravity. The result has instead exposed a nonstandard or state-dependent interface that requires direct investigation.
The same discipline applies to the complete process data. Before the local-classical comparison is trusted, the confidence-valid data must remain compatible with at least one physically admissible finite quantum process. If 𝓡α denotes the confidence region supported by the measurements and 𝒦Q the set of valid quantum processes over the tested input-output domain, the required consistency condition is
𝓡α ∩ 𝒦Q ≠ ∅.
This is not a test of quantum mechanics in unlimited generality. It is a local permission to use the finite process description required by the downstream inference. If the intersection is empty, the appropriate conclusion is that the assumed process representation is inadequate at the tested scope. The classical-process test is then suspended rather than used for further inference.
A second independent requirement is gravity attribution. The experiment must show that the feature tested in the process data changes in the manner expected when the gravitational interaction is present and is suppressed or altered in the corresponding control configuration. This requires more than agreement with a nominal Newtonian phase. Nongravitational channels capable of generating the same process feature have to be either physically suppressed, independently calibrated, or included quantitatively in the comparison.
The control therefore has to preserve as much of the apparatus as possible while changing the gravitational contribution in a predeclared way. The exact realization may be geometric, temporal, screened, or otherwise apparatus-specific. Its purpose is not to create a fictitious state in which gravity has disappeared. It is to produce a calibrated change in the target gravitational contribution while retaining enough of the remaining experimental architecture to determine whether the disputed process feature tracks gravity rather than an uncontrolled companion interaction.
This distinction becomes critical whenever an alternate quantum-information path exists. A matter-field process, electromagnetic leakage, shared quantum ancilla, optical coupling, or another quantum channel could produce a nonclassical endpoint process without requiring quantum information to have been carried by gravity. The experiment must therefore carry quantitative bounds on every such channel admitted at the scale relevant to the process witness. A bypass is not excluded because it appears intuitively small. Its maximum allowed contribution must be compared with the size of the effect required to explain the observed process data.[19]
Only after the finite quantum interface and gravity attribution survive does the standard process comparison become authoritative. Let 𝒦POUT denote a predeclared outer region that contains every endpoint process allowed by the admitted local classical-process class. The outer region may be larger than the exact LOCC set; indeed, using a larger region is conservative. If the experimentally allowed region is disjoint from it,
𝓡α ∩ 𝒦POUT = ∅,
then every process contained in that outer region is excluded at the declared confidence level, and therefore so is the narrower standard local classical-gravity process class.
The asymmetry of this test is important. Disjointness excludes the class. Intersection does not establish that the physical process is classical. It means only that the experiment has not excluded the class at the stated resolution. This keeps the inference aligned with what the data actually support.
The protocol must also establish that the quantum structure being protected by this analysis is physically relevant to the observed effect. For this reason the target data are paired with matched preparations in which the relevant coherence is deliberately removed while the path populations are retained. If the process feature survives deliberate dephasing unchanged within the experimental resolution, then quantum coherence has not been shown to be load-bearing for that feature. If, instead, the coherent and matched dephased preparations separate by more than a predeclared practical-equivalence margin, quantum structure has become experimentally necessary for the effect rather than a passive property of the apparatus.
These controls answer three different questions that should not be collapsed. The hidden-mixture test asks whether the ordinary finite quantum interface may be used. The coherent/dephased comparison asks whether quantum structure matters to the target effect. The gravity-suppressed comparison asks whether the target feature belongs to the gravitational interaction. Passing one of these tests does not supply the others.
Even successful exclusion of the standard local classical-process class does not finish the comparison. Section 3 identified alternatives that survive precisely by leaving that class. The same apparatus must therefore vary the physical coordinates on which those alternatives make distinct predictions.
Nonlocal or correlated classical models are particularly sensitive to this requirement. A single operating point may permit several models to reproduce the same phase, correlation, or entanglement value. Their evolution with separation and interaction time is much harder to disguise. The protocol therefore includes a predeclared finite scan through geometry and duration while retaining the same source–probe systems, the same interaction claim, and the same readout architecture. Diósi-Penrose-type models, for example, expose model-dependent separation thresholds and characteristic time evolution rather than merely predicting an undifferentiated presence or absence of entanglement.[20] More generally, the scan turns the target from one number into a trajectory.
The same trajectory supports the test of stochastic and irreversible classical alternatives. Local coherence, dephasing, diffusion, phase noise, and endpoint correlations are measured as functions of the same interaction time and geometry, with matched gravity-suppressed baselines. This is necessary because a loss of visibility in a mesoscopic interferometer is not, by itself, evidence for a gravitational noise mechanism. Environmental collisions, magnetic fluctuations, mechanical motion, imperfect recombination, surface interactions, and readout noise can all degrade coherence. A classical-gravity decoherence or diffusion bound becomes experimentally meaningful only after those ordinary sources have been measured or bounded in the same apparatus.
Where a classical-quantum theory predicts a relation between its interaction strength and an irreversibility cost, the comparison is made to that complete relation. The protocol does not infer “classical gravity” from excess noise, nor “quantum gravity” from unusually low noise. Instead, each admitted stochastic classical model receives its own allowed region in the joint process–coherence–noise data. Published decoherence–diffusion results and more recent correlation and fluctuation proposals provide examples of the kinds of relations that can make this branch experimentally finite.[21]
This is also why the experiment uses one common uncertainty object rather than a separate significance test for each alternative. The raw endpoint counts, calibration data, environmental monitors, geometry uncertainties, readout errors, and nuisance parameters are combined into one simultaneous confidence-valid region over the predeclared data set. Every exclusion statement is then evaluated against the same underlying experimental uncertainty.
The purpose is not merely statistical tidiness. If different alternatives were allowed to inherit different favorable selections of data, nuisance bounds, or confidence constructions after the outcome was visible, the comparison class could be narrowed by analysis choices rather than by physics. A common confidence region makes the relation explicit:
* data and calibrations
* → one confidence-valid experimental region
* → comparison with each predeclared physical feasible region.
The models themselves are subject to an analogous constraint. Each candidate classical representation is assigned its physical parameters and nuisance structure before adjudication and must use one allowed parameterization across every portion of the experiment it purports to explain. A model cannot fit the process signal with one gravitational parameter, the decoherence data with an incompatible value, and the geometry scan with a third unless those independent parameters were already part of the physical theory. Joint fit is part of faithfulness.
At the end of the protocol, the classical comparison has two layers. The first is direct experimental exclusion: the data rule out the standard local classical-process region and the separately admitted bypass, nonlocal, nonlinear, and stochastic alternatives. The second is coverage: the remaining comparison class must actually be broad enough to justify the intended conclusion. Experimental success cannot manufacture that coverage after the fact. The claim that the classical class has been exhausted requires an independently defensible account of which representations were admitted and why serious alternatives are either contained in the tested classes or routed to a separate test.
This final condition prevents a superficially strong experiment from becoming logically circular. The experimental data may exclude everything placed before them, but the conclusion “no admitted classical gravitational representation remains” is justified only if the admission rule itself was not chosen to guarantee that outcome. Conversely, the comparison class cannot be expanded without physical discipline merely to make exclusion impossible. A new classical proposal keeps the question open only when it supplies an independently specified representation of the same observed effect and survives the corresponding experimental requirements.
The complete adjudication can therefore be summarized without privileging any single observable. The experiment first establishes that the relevant quantum description is valid and load-bearing, and that the target process is gravitational. It then asks whether the observed endpoint process lies outside the standard local classical region. Any surviving explanation must expose the additional structure by which it escapes that region and survive the corresponding bypass, geometry-time, interface, or irreversibility test. All of these comparisons are made against one predeclared data package and one common uncertainty treatment.
If any required branch remains unresolved, classical-gravity representability remains open. If one admitted classical representation faithfully accounts for the full data package, the classical comparison survives. Only if the complete admitted class is excluded does the experiment reach the conclusion defined in Section 2: the same observations can no longer be reproduced by an adequate classical gravitational description, and a quantum-gravity resolution is required at that scope.
The next section specifies the finite data architecture needed to make this protocol executable.
5. Finite Data Architecture
The protocol described above does not require an unlimited characterization of the gravitational interaction. It requires a finite operational fragment large enough to test the assumptions used by the process inference, reconstruct the relevant endpoint dynamics, and expose the alternative classical explanations identified in Section 3. The present design uses two independently controlled massive systems, each supporting an effective two-path degree of freedom during the interaction.
For each system, denote the two localized path states by
|0⟩ ≡ |L⟩,
|1⟩ ≡ |R⟩.
The associated local preparation and readout library consists of the six Pauli eigenstates
Z⁺ = |L⟩,
Z⁻ = |R⟩,
X⁺ = (|L⟩ + |R⟩)/√2,
X⁻ = (|L⟩ − |R⟩)/√2,
Y⁺ = (|L⟩ + i|R⟩)/√2,
Y⁻ = (|L⟩ − i|R⟩)/√2.
Calling this degree of freedom a path qubit is operational shorthand. It does not assume that the complete massive object is fundamentally a two-level system. The experiment uses the controlled two-path subspace as the finite quantum degree of freedom on which preparations, interaction effects, and measurements are registered.
The primary process characterization does not require all six states as inputs. A tomographically spanning local input set is
𝒮T = {Z⁺, Z⁻, X⁺, Y⁺}.
Using the Cartesian product 𝒮T × 𝒮T gives sixteen product preparations for the two endpoints.[22] The outputs are measured in all nine joint combinations of local X, Y, and Z readout bases. Each joint basis produces four possible endpoint outcomes. One complete process block at a fixed interaction setting therefore contains
16 preparations × 9 joint readouts = 144 setting cells
before repeated trials within each cell.
This full block is the main process object. It permits reconstruction or direct witness evaluation over the finite source–probe process rather than reducing the experiment to one output state or one entanglement statistic. Existing process-level quantum-gravity proposals demonstrate that the relevant distinction can lie in the complete dynamics even when no entanglement is generated, which is why the present architecture retains the larger process object.[23]
The process block alone is not sufficient. The assumptions required to interpret it have to be tested using additional preparations constructed from the same endpoint controls.
The first such test uses hidden procedural mixtures. For endpoint A, consider three laboratory procedures:
EZᴬ = ½ Z⁺ + ½ Z⁻,
EXᴬ = ½ X⁺ + ½ X⁻,
EYᴬ = ½ Y⁺ + ½ Y⁻.
Under the ordinary quantum description all three procedures prepare the same maximally mixed path state. The distinction is entirely in how the state was prepared. The random choice used to construct the mixture is withheld from the interaction and from the other endpoint. Endpoint B is then cycled through the four tomographic input states in 𝒮T, and the joint outputs are measured in all nine Pauli-basis combinations.
This gives
3 hidden mixtures × 4 partner inputs × 9 readouts = 108 setting cells
for endpoint A. The procedure is then repeated with A and B exchanged, adding another 108 cells.
These runs do more than check ordinary preparation fidelity. They test whether physically distinct procedures that represent the same density operator remain operationally equivalent after the interaction. The complete output distributions generated by EZ, EX, and EY are compared simultaneously, not merely through one expectation value. The experiment therefore tests the preparation equivalence on the same interaction domain in which the process-classicality result will later be interpreted.[24]
If the hidden procedures remain equivalent within a predeclared tolerance and the complete process data remain compatible with at least one valid finite quantum process, the standard channel description is supported over the tested domain. If they do not, the downstream channel-class comparison is suspended. The experiment has then found a different problem: a preparation-dependent or otherwise nonstandard interface that has to be tested directly.
A second full 144-cell process block is acquired under a matched control in which the target mutual gravitational contribution is strongly suppressed or otherwise changed in a predeclared and calculable way. The preparation and readout library is unchanged. Wherever practical, the timing and local control sequence are also retained. The intention is to change the gravitational contribution while disturbing the surrounding apparatus as little as possible.
The precise implementation is platform-dependent. A useful control might alter the geometry, interaction duration, screening configuration, or another independently modeled parameter. What matters is that the control has a calculable relation to the target gravitational effect and does not simply replace it with a new uncontrolled interaction.
The target and suppressed blocks are therefore structurally parallel:
* target process block: 144 cells,
* gravity-suppressed process block: 144 cells.
Their difference does not by itself establish gravity attribution. Background and bypass calibrations remain necessary. The paired blocks instead provide the central process-level comparison on which those calibrations act.
A third control tests whether the quantum coherence of the endpoints is actually load-bearing. The primary coherent preparation is
X⁺A ⊗ X⁺B.
It is compared with a matched dephased preparation that retains the same path populations but removes the relevant coherence. Operationally, that control is implemented as a hidden randomization over the corresponding Z-basis path preparations rather than reconstructed afterward from other runs.
This distinction is important. Reconstructing the dephased result by averaging data from pure-state runs would already assume the linear mixture rule that the experiment is meant to test independently. The dephased preparation must therefore enter the interaction as its own registered laboratory procedure.
Both the coherent and matched dephased preparations are measured in all nine joint Pauli bases, giving eighteen explicit setting cells at the primary interaction point. The resulting difference is evaluated against a practical-equivalence margin fixed before the target data are inspected. If deliberate removal of coherence leaves the relevant process feature unchanged within that margin, the experiment has not shown that quantum coherence is necessary for the effect. If the coherent and dephased preparations separate beyond the predeclared margin after nuisance corrections, quantum structure has become experimentally load-bearing.
The target operating point is supplemented by a finite geometry-and-time trajectory. Let
𝒟 = {d₁, …, dK}
denote the predeclared set of interaction geometries or separations and
𝒯 = {t₁, …, tJ}
the corresponding set of interaction durations, with at least two distinct values in each coordinate. The exact numerical grid is chosen before the target measurements using the expected discrimination power against the admitted alternative model families.
At every pair (dk, tj), the experiment acquires the coherent X⁺A ⊗ X⁺B preparation and its matched dephased counterpart, again with all nine joint Pauli readouts. The scan therefore contains
2 preparation classes × 9 readouts × KJ points
= 18KJ setting cells.
This trajectory serves several experimental functions at once. It tests whether the target process scales with the expected gravitational interaction. It supplies the separation- and time-dependent information needed to discriminate nonlocal or correlated classical models. It measures local coherence and nonlocal correlations over the same trajectory. And it provides the data required to compare stochastic or irreversible classical-gravity alternatives against the observed noise and decoherence budget.
A corresponding gravity-suppressed temporal baseline is acquired over the same time values. Using the coherent and matched dephased preparations with all nine readout pairs contributes up to
18J additional setting cells.
The purpose of this baseline is particularly important for the irreversibility tests. A decrease in interference visibility with increasing hold time occurs in essentially every real mesoscopic experiment. Without a matched baseline, ordinary environmental decoherence could be mistaken for a model-specific gravitational noise cost. The control establishes how the endpoint coherence, phase noise, and correlation structure evolve when the target gravitational contribution has been suppressed according to the predeclared control model.
The remaining calibration family is necessarily apparatus-specific. Before target-data analysis, every admitted nongravitational or alternate quantum channel capable of contributing at the scale relevant to the process result is assigned a measurement or quantitative upper bound. Depending on the final implementation, this may include electromagnetic coupling, magnetic interactions, Casimir or van der Waals forces, patch potentials, optical leakage, substrate or mechanical coupling, residual gas, thermal effects, and matter-field quantum-information channels.
Each such background enters the experiment with the same required information: a calibration intervention or monitor, an allowed parameter range, a transfer model into the endpoint data, and a rule for extrapolating that calibration to the target configuration. No channel is declared negligible merely because an order-of-magnitude argument suggests that it should be small. Its allowed contribution has to be expressed in units that can be compared directly with the size of the process feature it might explain.
The result is one data object rather than a collection of preferred plots. For every registered setting r, the raw endpoint outcome counts
nr(a,b)
are retained together with the preparation identifier, readout settings, interaction geometry and duration, timestamps, detector status, randomization audit information, environmental monitors, position diagnostics, and the background-control channels declared before the experiment.
The analysis object can be written schematically as
𝒟* = endpoint counts
 + calibration data
 + environmental and apparatus monitors
 + declared missingness and failure records.
A setting is not removed merely because its result weakens the preferred interpretation. Hardware failure, corrupted acquisition, reruns, minimum valid counts, and missing-data treatment are all governed by rules fixed before the target data are opened. If missing data prevent construction of the required confidence-valid region, the corresponding scientific question remains unresolved rather than being repaired through outcome-dependent imputation.
The statistical analysis likewise operates on the complete registered object. Before target acquisition, the experiment fixes a global false-exclusion level α*. It then constructs one simultaneous confidence-valid region ℛα* covering the complete data cube and its declared nuisance parameters with coverage at least
1 − α*.
The precise statistical construction can be chosen during implementation. A calibrated likelihood-ratio region, a validated simultaneous bootstrap construction, or a sufficiently conservative exact method could each be appropriate. What is fixed by the present architecture is not one particular algorithm but the requirement that the same confidence object propagate the relevant counting, calibration, drift, geometry, readout, background, and monitor uncertainties into every downstream comparison.
This shared uncertainty region matters because the experiment asks several logically linked questions. If the process comparison used one favorable statistical treatment, the bypass analysis another, and the decoherence analysis a third, the apparent exhaustion of the classical class could be created partly by inconsistent error accounting. The common region instead preserves a single relation between the observed experiment and all tested physical alternatives.
The presently registered architecture therefore contains, before endpoint-certification runs, apparatus-specific background calibrations, and repeated shots,
* 108 hidden-mixture cells for endpoint A,
* 108 hidden-mixture cells for endpoint B,
* 144 target-process cells,
* 144 gravity-suppressed process cells,
* 18 coherent/dephased cells at the primary point,
* 18KJ geometry/time-scan cells,
* 18J gravity-suppressed temporal-baseline cells.
The symbolic total is
Ncells = 522 + 18KJ + 18J.[25]
This number is not presented as an information-theoretic minimum. It is the smallest presently defended architecture for which every scientific requirement identified in Sections 2–4 remains explicitly represented. A later implementation may reduce the preparation or measurement count if it can demonstrate that the relevant process discrimination, mixture-consistency test, gravity attribution, coherence dependence, alternative-model discrimination, and uncertainty coverage all survive the reduction.
The reason to retain the full architecture at this stage is methodological rather than aesthetic. The proposed experiment is intended to support a conclusion about a comparison class, not merely detect a small phase. Redundancy that independently checks the assumptions supporting that conclusion is scientifically useful until it has been shown to be unnecessary.
The different data families therefore form one connected measurement object. The hidden mixtures establish whether the finite quantum interface may be used. The coherent/dephased comparison establishes whether quantum structure matters to the observed effect. The paired target and gravity-suppressed process blocks establish the process-level contrast needed for gravity attribution and classical-process exclusion. The geometry/time trajectory forces nonlocal, correlated, and stochastic alternatives to reproduce more than one operating point. The background calibrations bound alternate channels capable of carrying the same apparent signal. And the single confidence region ensures that every comparison inherits the same accounting of experimental uncertainty.
No individual component closes the quantum-gravity question. Their force comes from conjunction. The experiment becomes decisive only when the same body of data simultaneously supports the quantum interface, the load-bearing role of quantum coherence, the gravity attribution, and the exclusion of every admitted classical representation under one physically consistent model fit.
The next question is whether this finite architecture can be realized in a physically coherent parameter regime. That requires moving from symbolic preparations and controls to masses, path separations, interaction distances, coherence times, environmental noise, and achievable apparatus performance.
6. Feasibility and the Current Measurement Gap
The protocol is useful only if its different requirements can coexist in one physically plausible apparatus family. A process signal large enough to discriminate classical alternatives is of little value if the same operating point destroys the endpoint coherence needed to interpret it. Conversely, exquisite coherence at masses too small to generate a resolvable gravitational process does not instantiate the experiment developed above. The feasibility question is therefore an overlap problem: whether mass, spatial delocalization, interaction distance, interaction time, environmental isolation, and readout precision can occupy one common regime.
Current screened and trapped mesoscopic-interferometer proposals provide a physically coherent starting point. Conducting or superconducting screening can reduce direct electromagnetic interactions between adjacent massive superpositions, while trapping can limit the trajectory distortions introduced by the screen itself.[26] A subsequent parameter scan incorporating electromagnetic screening places a favorable region near masses of order 10⁻¹⁴ kg, closest separations of order 35 μm, interaction times of order one second, and micrometre-scale spatial superpositions, with the required split increasing as decoherence worsens.[27] These numbers are proposal-level results rather than demonstrated operating specifications, but they locate a regime in which the different requirements of the present protocol are not obviously mutually incompatible.
The present feasibility calibration uses the nominal point
m = 10⁻¹⁴ kg,
d = 35 μm,
Δx = 8 μm,
τ = 1 s,
with a cryogenic environment near 4 K and a target total decoherence rate no larger than approximately 10⁻² Hz. These values are not part of the logical adjudication criterion. They are a candidate engineering point chosen to test whether the finite data architecture can plausibly be realized in the same experiment.
For the parallel two-path geometry used in the current screening analyses, the two gravitational branch-frequency contributions may be written
ω₁ = ω₂
= (Gm²/ℏ)[1/√(d² + Δx²) − 1/d].
The accumulated phase scale is therefore
ΦG = |ω₁ + ω₂|τ.
At the nominal point above,
ΦG ≈ 9.1 × 10⁻² rad.
For Δx ≪ d, the leading scaling is approximately
ΦG ≈ Gm²Δx²τ/(ℏd³).
The important conclusion is not the exact value of 0.09 rad. It is that the candidate regime is not signal-starved at the level of the bare Newtonian phase. The principal feasibility pressure moves instead to maintaining and characterizing the quantum state well enough that this scale can support the much stronger process comparison required here.[28]
The same scaling explains why a geometry-and-time trajectory is experimentally useful. Changing d and τ over a modest predeclared range changes the gravitational phase by much more than a simple repeated measurement at one point while keeping the physical source, probe, and interaction family fixed. A provisional scan with separations between roughly 35 and 75 μm and interaction times between roughly 0.5 and 2 s spans more than an order of magnitude in the expected phase. The final grid should be chosen only after the competing model families and apparatus transfer functions are numerically frozen, but the relevant leverage exists.
The more difficult question is whether the required spatial superposition can be produced and recombined at the target mass. Here the current experimental frontier and the current proposal frontier remain distinct.
Published microchip-trap designs target nanodiamonds of order 10⁻¹⁵ kg and micrometre-scale superpositions, with fast switching and integrated superconducting shielding.[29] Inverted-harmonic-potential proposals extend the theoretical reach further: one published design obtains a roughly 50 μm split for a 10⁻¹⁵ kg object in about 0.1 s, while a more recent two-dimensional treatment based on superconducting-chip field profiles obtains approximately 4.4 μm in 0.5 s for the same mass scale.[30] These studies establish that the control architecture is moving into the spatial and temporal range required by the present proposal.
They do not yet establish the target point. The nominal experiment asks for an object roughly one decade heavier, near 10⁻¹⁴ kg, while retaining several micrometres of coherent path separation and a controlled split–hold–recombine sequence lasting approximately a second. No experiment currently demonstrates that combined mass, separation, and coherence requirement.
The distinction is particularly visible when compared with direct nanoparticle matter-wave interferometry. Pedalino et al. have demonstrated interference of sodium nanoparticles with masses above 170,000 Da, corresponding to approximately 2.8 × 10⁻²² kg.[31] That experiment is not the same architecture as a trapped spin-dependent nanodiamond interferometer, so the mass ratio should not be interpreted as a single technological ladder that must be climbed by one method. It nevertheless establishes the demonstrated center-of-mass interference frontier against which the proposed 10⁻¹⁴ kg path superposition should be described. The target remains a substantial experimental advance, not an already realized capability.
Residual-gas decoherence is the sharpest quantitative environmental requirement identified in the present calibration. In the short-wavelength limit for collisional decoherence of a spherical nanodiamond, the relevant rate can be written
ΓSWL = 4πℏngR²/(mgλth),
with
ng = P/(kBT).
Applying the published residual-gas model to a diamond of mass 10⁻¹⁴ kg at 4 K gives a radius of approximately 0.88 μm. Requiring gas scattering alone to remain below 10⁻² Hz corresponds, under an ideal nitrogen-gas conversion, to an equivalent pressure of approximately
P ≲ 4 × 10⁻¹⁵ Pa.
Pushing the collisional contribution toward 10⁻³ Hz lowers the corresponding ideal-gas value by another order of magnitude.[32]
These pressure values should not be mistaken for a simple vacuum-gauge specification. At cryogenic temperature, the local species distribution, cryosorption, conductance, desorption, nearby surfaces, and departures from an ideal homogeneous gas become important. The scientifically relevant quantity is the measured local collision-induced decoherence rate of the actual interferometer, together with the transfer from that rate into the registered process data. The equivalent pressure is useful because it shows the severity of the requirement, not because pressure alone is the final observable.
This requirement is consistent with independent analyses of Feynman-type gravity experiments, which identify residual-gas scattering near 10⁻¹⁵ Pa as a formidable requirement for approximately 10⁻¹⁴ kg objects evolving coherently for multiple seconds.[33] The present one-second target helps, but it does not remove the vacuum problem.
Spin coherence presents a different kind of gap. In isotopically engineered bulk diamond, a 2026 preprint reports single-NV electron-spin coherence of T₂DD = 11.2(8) s under tailored dynamical decoupling.[34] This demonstrates that the electronic spin itself can, in a sufficiently controlled diamond environment, remain coherent well beyond the nominal interaction time. Existing nanodiamond results are substantially shorter: published measurements in milled nanodiamonds reach hundreds of microseconds under dynamical decoupling and related control sequences.[35]
The target object lies between those regimes. A roughly micrometre-scale high-purity diamond should not simply be assumed to inherit the bulk result, but neither is it identical to a sub-100-nm milled nanodiamond dominated by surface noise. The required transfer—second-scale spin coherence in the actual trapped object while it undergoes the interferometric sequence—remains a measurement target. That is a materially different statement from saying that the needed spin coherence is unknown in principle.
Magnetic control introduces another explicit requirement. The same strong field gradients and inverted harmonic potentials that accelerate spatial separation make the trajectories sensitive to fluctuations in the applied magnetic field. A 2026 analysis of an inverted-harmonic-potential nanodiamond interferometer found, for its chosen 10⁻¹⁵ kg and approximately micrometre-scale configuration, that maintaining the declared coherence criterion could require relative curvature stability at the level of about 10⁻¹³ for the inverted potential.[36] That number is model- and apparatus-specific and should not be copied directly into the final experiment specification. It demonstrates that magnetic-noise accounting cannot be treated as a secondary engineering detail.
The electromagnetic screen likewise solves one problem by creating others that must be calibrated. It suppresses direct electrostatic, magnetic, dipole, and Casimir-mediated interactions between the two test masses, but introduces surface forces, image effects, possible dephasing, superconducting or magnetic noise, and additional geometry dependence. The advantage of the screened architecture is not that those effects disappear. It is that they become local apparatus interactions that can, in principle, be measured independently and incorporated into the background model. Integrated superconducting shielding and rapidly switchable microfabricated traps are attractive for this reason.[37]
The alternative quantum-information bypass identified in Section 3 does not presently eliminate the candidate window. In the specific QFT-matter calculation of Aziz and Howl, the classical-gravity matter-sector contribution is substantially smaller than the leading quantum-gravity phase around M ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ kg and t ≈ 2 s, while becoming more important as the mass approaches the Planck scale and the interaction time is shortened.[38] The nominal one-second, 10⁻¹⁴ kg regime therefore lies in a comparatively favorable region for that particular bypass. This is not an exclusion result. It means only that the first serious matter-sector bypass already identified in the literature is not expected to overwhelm the target effect in the present candidate regime.
There is also no reason to assume that the optimum experiment must remain at the smallest possible mass. The gravitational phase grows approximately as m², while short-wavelength residual-gas scattering for a fixed-density spherical object grows only as approximately m²⁄³ at fixed pressure. If future control methods can preserve a multi-micrometre superposition at larger mass, a heavier object operated for a much shorter time may improve the ratio between gravitational signal and accumulated environmental decoherence. The current 10⁻¹⁴ kg point should therefore be treated as a defensible baseline, not as a theoretically privileged optimum.
What remains unavailable is the final process-level power calculation. A 0.09-rad gravitational phase establishes useful signal scale, but the proposed experiment does not adjudicate quantum gravity by measuring that phase alone. The relevant question is how far the resulting confidence-valid process data can be driven outside the conservative classical-process region once finite sampling, preparation error, readout error, decoherence, geometry uncertainty, and calibrated bypass contributions are propagated together. Until the numerical outer process region and shot allocation are constructed, the required number of experimental repetitions remains open.
This is the point at which the measurement gap becomes scientifically useful. The proposed experiment does not require specialists to accept a new microscopic picture of gravity. It asks them to close a finite set of experimental quantities to stated standards: prepare and recombine the required mesoscopic path states; measure their mixture consistency; keep the local collision-induced decoherence below the registered budget; characterize spin and motional coherence in the actual target object; calibrate magnetic and screen-induced disturbances; bound alternate quantum channels; resolve the geometry-and-time trajectory; and determine the finite-sample distance between the observed process and the admitted classical-process region.
If those tasks cannot be achieved at the nominal point, the logical criterion does not fail. The apparatus point changes. A higher-mass, shorter-duration configuration, a different trapping architecture, or an alternative matter-wave platform may satisfy the same experimental requirements. If no physically credible implementation can satisfy the required experimental conditions simultaneously, then the proposed experiment has exposed its own feasibility limit rather than converting that limit into a physical conclusion.
The present status is therefore specific. The full experiment has not been demonstrated. Several of its component capabilities have been demonstrated at smaller mass, shorter coherence, or in different apparatus classes. Published and preprint apparatus studies support a regime in which the required gravitational phase, screening, and spatial control are physically coherent as a common target. The numerical calibration developed here identifies a representative point near 10⁻¹⁴ kg, 35 μm interaction separation, an 8 μm path split, and a one-second interaction, while locating residual-gas decoherence, target-mass motional coherence, spin-coherence transfer, magnetic/screen noise, and final process-witness power as the principal unresolved measurements.
Those unresolved measurements are not ancillary to the result. They are the remaining empirical work by which the adjudication can be completed.
The next section addresses what a successful completion of that work would establish—and, equally importantly, what it would leave undecided. 
7. Interpretation: What the Experiment Would Establish
The experiment developed in the preceding sections is designed to support one specific inference. A successful result would not identify the microscopic theory of quantum gravity. It would establish that, for the tested observations and declared physical scope, no classical gravitational description included under the predeclared comparison rule remains adequate.
That conclusion is stronger than the failure of a particular semiclassical model and weaker than the selection of a quantum-gravity ontology. Before it is available, the source and probe must support the finite quantum description used in the analysis; the relevant quantum coherence must be shown to matter to the measured effect; the effect must survive the registered gravity-attribution tests and background bounds; the standard local classical-process class must be excluded; the serious alternatives that escape that class must fail their corresponding discriminators; and the coverage of the classical comparison class must be justified independently of the outcome.
When those conditions hold together, the inference is
no included classical gravitational representation remains adequate
⇒ quantum-gravity resolution required at the declared scope.
“Quantum-gravity resolution” is intentionally representation-neutral. It does not say that gravity is thereby shown to be a quantum field, nor does it establish gravitons, quantum geometry, discrete spacetime, a persistent metric, or any other particular microscopic ontology. A successor description might quantize a gravitational field, reorganize the matter–geometry distinction, introduce different quantum gravitational degrees of freedom, or take another form entirely. The experiment does not choose among those possibilities. It establishes only that an adequate description of the tested observations can no longer remain within the classical comparison class that has been excluded.
This also distinguishes the result from the weaker statement that an observation is “consistent with quantum gravity.” Consistency can be shared by many incompatible models. The present criterion depends instead on exclusion: the same data that support the target effect must also rule out the included classical ways of reproducing it.
An unresolved branch therefore remains scientifically consequential even when the primary signal is strong. If the measured endpoint process lies outside the standard local classical region but an alternate quantum-information path has not been bounded below the required effect size, the gravity-specific inference remains open. If a nonlocal classical model reproduces the complete geometry-and-time trajectory, classical representability survives. If the target feature tracks an uncontrolled background rather than the gravitational intervention, gravity attribution fails. If the hidden preparation mixtures cease to be operationally equivalent, the ordinary linear-channel analysis is no longer supported over the tested domain.
The converse matters as well. Compatibility with the classical-process region does not prove that gravity is classical. It means only that the experiment has not excluded that class at the achieved sensitivity. The protocol therefore distinguishes classical survival, unresolved comparison, and classical exclusion. Only the last, together with the independently established quantum and gravity premises and the justified coverage of the comparison class, supports the quantum-gravity conclusion.
No finite experiment can enumerate every theory that might ever be proposed. The coverage claim is narrower. A classical representation belongs in the comparison when it supplies a physically specified account of the same tested effect and satisfies the declared rules of scope, faithfulness, and empirical contact. If a later theory meeting those conditions reproduces the complete registered data, classical representability genuinely reopens.[39] If a formally novel theory falls within an already tested physical class and fails the same discriminator, mathematical novelty alone does not reopen the result.
The conclusion is therefore intentionally revisable. Improved calibration may show that an earlier gravity attribution was mistaken. Stronger process reconstruction may restore compatibility with a classical region. Better controls may instead exclude alternatives that are presently viable. The status of the inference changes when the physical evidence supporting its premises changes.
This scope dependence is not in tension with the continued empirical success of General Relativity. Demonstrating the inadequacy of a classical gravitational description for one controlled mesoscopic quantum interaction would locate a boundary of classical adequacy; it would not invalidate the classical theory in regimes where it remains successful.
The accompanying formal contract has a narrower role still. It does not establish any empirical premise.[40] It checks the conditional inference linking the declared premises to the scoped quantum-gravity conclusion and verifies the two principal firewalls: experimental subtests alone do not establish exhaustion of the full classical comparison class, and exhaustion of that class does not select a particular quantum-gravity ontology.[41]
The physics artifact and formal contract therefore divide the work cleanly. The physics artifact defines the systems, measurements, comparison classes, controls, uncertainty rules, and empirical criteria. The machine artifact checks that the resulting conditional logic says no more than those premises support.
If the experiment ultimately excludes the complete predeclared classical comparison class while the quantum and gravity conditions remain satisfied, then the question “could an adequate classical gravitational description still reproduce these observations?” has received a scoped empirical answer.
At that point, a quantum-gravity resolution would be required.
The experiment would still not say what that resolution is.
8. Conclusion
The experimental question addressed in this paper is not whether quantum systems can participate in gravitational interactions. They can. Nor is it whether one classical model can fail, whether gravity can generate an unusual phase, or whether an experiment can produce entanglement. The relevant question is stronger: what evidence would establish that the same gravitational effect and associated data can no longer be reproduced by an adequate classical description?
Answering that question requires more than one privileged signature. The quantum degree of freedom used in the inference must first be independently established. Its quantum structure must be shown to matter to the observed effect. The effect must survive a genuine gravity-attribution test. Only then does the decisive comparison become available: whether any admitted classical gravitational representation can still reproduce the complete registered data.
The resulting criterion is
* independently established quantum structure
* ∧ quantum structure load-bearing for the tested effect
* ∧ independent gravity attribution
* ∧ failure of every admitted classical gravitational representation
* ⇒ quantum-gravity resolution required at the declared scope.
The principal difficulty lies in the final term. Classical gravity cannot be identified with one semiclassical mean-field model, one metric formulation, or one local mediator picture. Current theory already supplies serious alternatives involving stochastic dynamics, nonlocal or correlated classical structure, measurement-conditioned gravity, modified quantum interfaces, and quantum-information bypasses through matter. A decisive experiment therefore has to test the ways those theories escape the standard local classical-process class rather than treating them as after-the-fact loopholes.
The proposed protocol does this with one bounded experimental family. It tests preparation consistency before invoking a linear process description, establishes whether quantum coherence is load-bearing, compares target and gravity-suppressed process data, bounds alternate quantum channels, scans geometry and interaction time, measures the accompanying decoherence and noise trajectory, and evaluates the resulting data against one common uncertainty treatment. The claim is made only if one physically consistent classical representation cannot account for the complete registered data.
That program is finite. In its present form, the core process architecture contains a fixed preparation and measurement library together with explicitly registered mixture, control, trajectory, and baseline data. Its feasibility calibration identifies a candidate regime near 10⁻¹⁴ kg masses, tens-of-micrometres interaction separation, several-micrometre path splitting, and approximately second-scale interaction times. The bare gravitational phase is not prohibitively small in that regime. The unresolved work lies instead in achieving and characterizing the required mesoscopic coherence, residual-gas environment, magnetic and surface backgrounds, spin-coherence transfer, alternate-channel bounds, and final process-level statistical power.
Those are not unspecified future difficulties. They are measurable quantities. Closing them would convert the present protocol from a proposed adjudication into an experimental result.
A successful result would have a precise meaning. It would establish that the admitted classical gravitational descriptions are inadequate for the same quantum-mechanical observations at the tested scope. It would therefore make a quantum-gravity resolution physically required there.
It would not establish which quantum-gravity theory is correct. Nothing in the result alone selects gravitons, quantum geometry, discreteness, strings, loops, a persistent metric, or any other microscopic ontology. Those questions remain for subsequent theory and experiment. The achievement would be prior to them: establishing experimentally that a classical gravitational answer is no longer sufficient.
The conclusion would also remain scientifically reversible. A newly demonstrated classical representation capable of faithfully reproducing the same complete data would reopen the comparison. Improved calibration capable of defeating the gravity attribution would reopen it as well. Conversely, stronger experiments may close alternatives that presently remain viable. The status of the quantum-gravity requirement therefore follows the evidence supporting its premises rather than becoming insulated from revision.
Quantum gravity has long been posed primarily as a problem of theory construction. The present work isolates a complementary empirical problem: determining the conditions under which nature itself requires that construction to become quantum.
The proposed experiment does not answer what quantum gravity is.
It defines how to determine when a quantum-gravity answer has become experimentally necessary.
